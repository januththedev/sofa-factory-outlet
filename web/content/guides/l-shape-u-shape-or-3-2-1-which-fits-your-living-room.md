An L-shape is the best fit for most living rooms because it fills a corner and leaves the centre free, a U-shape suits bigger rooms built around conversation, and a 3+2+1 set is the flexible choice for long, narrow or multi-use spaces. The right shape is decided by the room first — measurements, walkways, doors and where the TV sits — and only then by taste.

## Quick answer

- **Measure first.** All four walls, every door and the way it swings, the TV wall, and the path people walk every day.

- **U-shape** gives generous seating for hosting, but needs clear space on three sides.

## Start with the room, not the sofa

Before you fall for any shape, measure the room properly. Write down the length of each wall, note where the doors are and which way they swing, mark the windows, and mark the wall the TV sits on. A sofa that blocks a door swing or leaves everyone craning towards the screen is wrong however good it looks.

Then trace the walkways. People walk the same routes every day — from the door to the kitchen, past the sofa to the window — and those routes need to stay open. As general guidance, keep about a metre clear for a main path past the sofa and about half a metre between the sofa and a coffee table, so nobody shuffles sideways or stubs a toe. Clearance matters at the back too: leave a little air between the sofa and the wall so the room stays fresh.

Think about the view as well. Where you sit should face the television or the conversation, not the back of a door or a blank wall. A simple trick costs nothing: mark the sofa's footprint on the floor with masking tape and live with it for a day. If you keep walking into the marked corner, that shape is wrong for the room.

## The three shapes

### L-shape: the corner-saver

The L-shape — a sofa with a chaise or corner section — uses the corner that other furniture leaves empty, which is why it suits most living rooms and small flats so well. One long seat gives you somewhere to stretch out, and the middle of the room stays free for a table, for children to play or for the dog to stretch out. It works in a medium room, in a corner of an open-plan space, and anywhere the television sits on one of the two walls the sofa faces. The trade-off is the long arm: in a narrow room it can squeeze the walkway, so measure the route past it before you decide. See the [L-shape sofas](/sofas/l-shape/) we make.

### U-shape: the hosting shape

The U-shape wraps seating around three sides, so everyone faces everyone. It is built for conversation and for long evenings with guests, and it turns the sofa into the centre of the room rather than one wall's edge. The trade-off is space: a U-shape needs clear floor on three sides and a fair amount of wall, so it belongs in a larger living room. In a medium room it can leave awkward gaps and make the walkways tight. If hosting is what your living room is for, and the room can hold it, this is the shape. Browse the [U-shape sofas](/sofas/u-shape/) to see the footprint.

### 3+2+1: the flexible classic

A 3+2+1 set is three separate pieces — a three-seater, a two-seater and a single — arranged however the room allows. You can set them along two walls, split them between rooms, or move a piece when guests come. That flexibility makes it a good match for long, narrow rooms, for households whose needs change, and for anyone who prefers a traditional layout to one big corner piece. The three pieces together need more wall length than a single sofa, so add the widths before you commit. The [sofa sets](/sofas/sets/) we make cover this classic layout.

## Everyday life: entertaining, small flats, kids and pets

Entertaining and everyday living pull in different directions. If your evenings are guests and conversation, the U-shape's wrap-around seating earns the space it takes. If most nights are one or two people watching television, an L-shape gives the same comfort with a smaller footprint. A 3+2+1 sits in the middle: formal enough for visitors, easy enough for everyday.

Small flats rarely have a spare metre. An L-shape tucks into the corner you were not using anyway, and a 3+2+1 can run along a long wall without eating the middle of the room. A U-shape usually asks for more than a flat can give.

Children and pets change the maths as well. A layout that leaves a clear route past the sofa means nobody trips over someone playing on the floor, and a shape with room to walk behind it makes vacuuming easier. If a pet has claimed one seat, life is simpler when a single piece can be moved or cleaned on its own.

## Common questions

### Which sofa shape suits a small living room?

An L-shape usually, because it uses a corner and keeps the middle of the room free. A 3+2+1 along one wall also works if the room is long and narrow.

### How much space should I leave around the sofa?

Keep about a metre for a main walking route past the sofa and about half a metre between the sofa and a coffee table. Check the door swings as well.

### Which shape is best when I host guests?

A U-shape, if the room can hold it, because the seating wraps around three sides and everyone faces each other. An L-shape is the everyday alternative.

### Is a U-shape too big for a medium room?

It often is. A U-shape needs clear space on three sides, so measure first and compare its footprint with the room before you decide.

### Do I need exact measurements before I book a visit?

Exact numbers are not required, but measurements help a lot. Bring the room's length, width and door positions, and the team can talk through what fits.
